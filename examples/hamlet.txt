Hamlet, Prince of Denmark — by William Shakespeare

Prince Hamlet of Denmark returns from university to find his father, King Hamlet, 
recently dead and his mother Gertrude hastily married to his uncle Claudius, who 
has taken the throne.

The ghost of Hamlet's father appears to him, revealing that Claudius murdered him 
by pouring poison in his ear while he slept. The ghost demands revenge.

Hamlet is torn. He's brilliant but paralyzed by indecision — he can't simply act, 
he must analyze, doubt, and verify. He decides to feign madness to investigate 
without suspicion.

Meanwhile, the court counselor Polonius believes Hamlet's madness stems from 
rejected love for his daughter Ophelia. Polonius and Claudius spy on Hamlet 
constantly.

Hamlet devises a test: he stages a play that mirrors his father's murder, 
watching Claudius's reaction. Claudius reacts with guilt — confirming everything.

But Hamlet still hesitates. When he finally acts, it's impulsive: he stabs 
through a curtain, killing Polonius instead of Claudius. This accident destroys 
everything — Ophelia goes mad with grief and drowns. Her brother Laertes returns, 
furious, and Claudius manipulates him into challenging Hamlet to a rigged duel 
with a poisoned blade.

At the final duel, everything unravels: Gertrude accidentally drinks poisoned 
wine meant for Hamlet. Laertes wounds Hamlet with the poisoned sword, but they 
exchange weapons and Hamlet wounds Laertes too. Dying, Laertes reveals Claudius's 
treachery. Hamlet finally kills Claudius, then dies.

Only Hamlet's loyal friend Horatio survives. He promises to tell the true story 
of what happened, ensuring the truth outlives the tragedy.

Key themes: the paralysis of overthinking, performance vs reality (everyone is 
acting a role), corruption spreading from the top, surveillance and paranoia, 
the cycle of revenge consuming the avenger, loyalty tested to destruction.
